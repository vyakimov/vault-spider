## Central Themes

Dostoevsky explores the corrosive power of isolation and the human capacity for rationalization. Raskolnikov's theory that the extraordinary can transcend morality is seductive but hollow—his crime does not liberate him but imprisons him in fever, guilt, and psychological torment. The novel argues that individual will cannot justify transgression of human connection; guilt is not merely legal but metaphysical.

Sonya's Christian faith offers an alternative: that redemption comes not through intellectual superiority but through suffering, love, and community. The famous passage where Raskolnikov asks Sonya to read the Lazarus story is pivotal—resurrection through faith rather than reason. The novel's arc moves from Raskolnikov's Napoleonic fantasy to his acceptance that salvation lies in acknowledgment of sin and human solidarity. Redemption requires not just intellectual assent but willingness to suffer and be loved.
