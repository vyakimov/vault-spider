Notes on anarchism, scarcity, and walls as the book's throughlines.

## Central Themes

Le Guin constructs a sophisticated anarchist thought-experiment in Anarres while demonstrating that ideology alone cannot resolve the tension between individual desire and collective need. Anarres was founded to escape the walls and hierarchies of capitalism, yet walls emerge there too—bureaucratic, social, and intellectual. Sabul's control over scientific collaboration mirrors capitalist gatekeeping; the Syndicate's consensus becomes authoritarian.

The novel's central paradox is that both worlds—the anarchist Anarres and the capitalist Urras—are "dispossessed" in different ways. Anarres has freed itself from private property but faces scarcity, conformity, and stifled ambition. Urras has freedom and resources but concentrates them in the hands of a few, leaving most dispossessed of dignity. Shevek's journey is neither a rejection of anarchism nor of individual achievement but a recognition that both require constant struggle, compromise, and above all human connection. His unified theory of time becomes a metaphor for unity that respects difference—not uniformity but genuine solidarity. The novel suggests that freedom and community are in perpetual tension, and any stable solution is temporary.
