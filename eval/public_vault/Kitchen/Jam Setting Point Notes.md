# Jam Setting Point Notes

## Cold-Plate Test (No Thermometer)

Chill a ceramic plate in the freezer before you start cooking. When you think the jam is ready (after 15–20 minutes of a rolling boil), drop a spoonful onto the plate, let it cool for 30 seconds, then push with your finger. If the surface wrinkles and does not flow back, the jam has reached setting point (approximately 220°F). If it's still runny, continue boiling and test again every 2–3 minutes.

## Thermometer Method

If using a candy or jam thermometer, clip it to the side of the pot so it does not touch the bottom. Setting point is 220–222°F at sea level (adjust 1°F lower for every 1,000 feet of elevation). For jam, you want a spreadable consistency—just past the gel point—so aim for 220°F. High-pectin fruits reach setting point faster (15–20 minutes); low-pectin fruits like strawberries may need 25–30 minutes or added pectin.

## Wrinkle Test Technique

The wrinkle test is the most reliable for home jam without special equipment. A good wrinkle means the jam has concentrated enough and will set properly once cooled. If you over-cook and the jam becomes too stiff, add a splash of lemon juice or water and reheat gently to soften it back. Once jarred and cooled completely (at least 12 hours), a proper set jam will wobble slightly but hold its shape when spooned.

## Sugar and Pectin Balance

Most jam recipes use a 1:1 ratio of fruit to sugar by weight, though low-sugar versions use less. Pectin content matters: berries are naturally high-pectin (blackberries, gooseberries); stone fruit and citrus are lower. A touch of lemon juice raises acidity and helps pectin gel. For a jammy, less-firm preserve, reduce cooking time or use less sugar; for a stiff, spreadable jam, cook longer or add powdered pectin (following package instructions).
